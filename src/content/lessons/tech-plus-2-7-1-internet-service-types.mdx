## Connecting the Local Network to the World

Inside a home or corporate office, devices communicate at blistering speeds over local gigabit Ethernet switches and Wi-Fi access points.

But to access cloud databases, web servers, video streams, and remote corporate branches, that local network must connect to an **Internet Service Provider (ISP)** over a **Wide Area Network (WAN)** connection.

As an IT technician, understanding the physical mediums, speed profiles, and latency characteristics of each broadband technology allows you to recommend the best WAN solution for any geographic location.

## 1. Wired Broadband: Fiber vs. Cable vs. DSL

### Fiber Optic (The Gold Standard)
- **Physics:** Transmits binary data as pulses of light through flexible glass or plastic fiber strands.
- **Symmetric Speeds:** Fiber delivers equal upload and download speeds (e.g. 1,000 Mbps download **and** 1,000 Mbps upload). This is critical for remote workers uploading 4K video or organizations hosting local backup servers.
- **Immunity:** Completely immune to electromagnetic interference (EMI), electrical lightning surges, and radio static.

### Cable Internet (DOCSIS)
- **Physics:** Delivers internet over the same RG-6 coaxial copper cables used by television networks using the **DOCSIS (Data Over Cable Service Interface Specification)** standard.
- **Asymmetric:** High download speeds (e.g. 500 Mbps), but limited upload speeds (e.g. 20 Mbps).
- **The Shared Node:** Cable bandwidth is shared across a neighborhood coaxial branch. If 40 neighbors begin streaming 4K movies at 8:00 PM, available bandwidth drops and latency increases.

### DSL (Digital Subscriber Line)
- **Physics:** Transmits high-frequency digital signals over the traditional twisted-pair copper telephone wires already running into homes.
- **Distance Limitation:** DSL speeds drop dramatically the farther the subscriber is physically located from the telephone company's **Central Office (CO) / DSLAM**. If you are 3 miles away, speeds can drop from 50 Mbps down to 3 Mbps.

## 2. Satellite Broadband: The Space Physics of Latency

When terrestrial cables (fiber, cable, DSL) are not available in remote rural, maritime, or wilderness locations, satellite broadband provides connectivity:

```
┌────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                        GEO VS. LEO SATELLITE                           │
├──────────────────────────────────┬─────────────────────────────────────┤
│ **Traditional GEO Satellites**   │ **Modern LEO Satellites**           │
│ *(HughesNet, Viasat)*            │ *(Starlink, Amazon Kuiper)*         │
├──────────────────────────────────┼─────────────────────────────────────┤
│ 🛰️ Altitude: **22,236 Miles**   │ 🛰️ Altitude: **340 Miles**         │
│ Speed of Light Round-Trip:       │ Speed of Light Round-Trip:          │
│ Ground $\rightarrow$ Orbit $\rightarrow$ Ground $\rightarrow$ Orbit │ Ground $\rightarrow$ Orbit $\rightarrow$ Ground │
│ ❌ **Latency: 600ms – 800ms**     │ ✔ **Latency: 25ms – 45ms**          │
│ (Unusable for Zoom & Gaming)     │ (Works for Real-Time Voice/Video)   │
└──────────────────────────────────┴─────────────────────────────────────┘
```

<Callout type="exam">
**The Latency Rule:** The speed of light is roughly 186,000 miles per second. Traveling 22,236 miles up to a geostationary satellite and back down twice takes roughly half a second of raw travel time alone. This physical barrier makes GEO satellite unusable for interactive VoIP calls and real-time remote desktops.
</Callout>

## 3. Cellular & Fixed Wireless Access (FWA)

- **Cellular Broadband (4G LTE / 5G):** Uses cellular SIM cards inside mobile hot-spots or enterprise backup routers. When an office fiber line is cut by a construction backhoe, enterprise routers automatically failover to a 5G cellular WAN connection.
- **Fixed Wireless Access (FWA):** A local ISP mounts a directional radio dish on the customer's roof pointed directly at a transmission tower on a nearby hill. Provides high-speed broadband across rural valleys without having to dig expensive underground trenches.

---

## Why This Matters in the Field

When a business opens a new branch office, the network engineer must choose the WAN connectivity:
- A primary **Symmetric Fiber link** for daily operations.
- A secondary **Cable or 5G backup link** configured for automatic failover.

Understanding WAN service types ensures you keep your organization connected regardless of geography or infrastructure outages.
