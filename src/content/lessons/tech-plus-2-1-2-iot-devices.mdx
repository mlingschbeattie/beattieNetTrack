## When Physical Objects Join the Network

Twenty years ago, if you wanted to know if a door was unlocked, you walked over and turned the handle. If a farmer wanted to check soil moisture in an apple orchard, they walked into the dirt with a shovel. If a factory manager wanted to know if a turbine bearing was wearing out, they waited until it started squealing and caught fire.

Today, all of those physical objects are connected to IP networks. This is the **Internet of Things (IoT)**.

An IoT device is any physical object embedded with sensors, microcontrollers, and wireless connectivity that can collect data, make autonomous decisions, and exchange telemetry over the internet without human intervention.

## Sensors vs. Actuators: Inputs and Outputs in the Real World

Every IoT device interacts with the physical world through two core components:

1. **Sensors (The Eyes and Ears — INPUT):** Convert physical analog properties from the environment into digital binary data streams.
   - *Photocells:* Measure ambient lumen light levels.
   - *Thermocouples / Thermistors:* Measure temperature in server rooms or refrigeration units.
   - *Accelerometers & Piezoelectric Sensors:* Detect mechanical vibration on factory motors.
   - *PIR (Passive Infrared):* Detect human body heat and motion in security systems.

2. **Actuators (The Hands and Feet — OUTPUT):** Receive digital network commands from a server or microcontroller and convert them into physical mechanical movement or electrical switching.
   - *Solenoids & Relays:* Mechanically trip electrical switches, open sprinkler valves, or fire door strikes.
   - *Servo Motors:* Rotate robotic arms, aim motorized PTZ security cameras, or open HVAC dampers.

## IoT Wireless and Messaging Protocols

Standard Wi-Fi and Ethernet are power-hungry. A battery-powered soil sensor buried in a cornfield cannot afford to run a full 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi radio that drains a battery in 48 hours.

To solve this, IoT relies on specialized, lightweight protocols:

| Protocol | Range & Frequency | Power Profile | Topology & Best Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Zigbee** | Short Range (~10–100m) / 2.4 GHz | Ultra-Low Power | **Mesh Topology:** Smart bulbs and smart plugs act as repeaters to blanket a building. |
| **Z-Wave** | Short Range (~30–100m) / 908 MHz | Ultra-Low Power | **Mesh Topology:** Sub-1GHz frequency avoids crowded 2.4GHz Wi-Fi interference. |
| **BLE (Bluetooth LE)** | Short Range (~10m) / 2.4 GHz | Tiny Coin-Cell Battery | **Point-to-Point:** Fitness trackers, continuous glucose monitors, asset tracking beacons. |
| **LoRaWAN** | Long Range (~5–15 km) / Sub-GHz | Multi-Year Battery | **Wide Area Star:** Agricultural sensors, smart water meters, municipal flood monitors. |
| **MQTT** | Application Layer Protocol | Minimal Overhead | **Publish-Subscribe:** Lightweight TCP protocol designed for unreliable network telemetry. |

## Enterprise IoT and Industrial SCADA Systems

In commercial and municipal infrastructure, IoT scales up to **Industrial Control Systems (ICS)** and **Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition (SCADA)**:

- **Predictive Maintenance:** Vibration sensors attached to cooling pumps on a naval ship or factory floor monitor frequencies in real time. When bearing wear creates a microscopic 120 Hz wobble, the system alerts technicians to schedule a 30-minute bearing replacement *before* the pump seizes and causes a half-million-dollar production shutdown.
- **SCADA Infrastructure:** Municipal water treatment plants, regional electrical power grids, oil pipelines, and railway switching networks rely on SCADA networks to monitor reservoir levels, adjust pipeline pressure, and trip circuit breakers automatically.

## The Dark Side: IoT Security & The Mirai Botnet

Because consumer IoT manufacturers prioritize low cost (15 smart bulbs,20 webcams), security is routinely neglected:
- Devices ship with hardcoded, unchangeable root passwords (`admin / admin`, `root / 123456`).
- Budget vendors rarely publish security firmware updates, leaving known vulnerabilities unpatched for years.
- Devices constantly open outbound UPnP connections to foreign cloud servers.

### The Mirai Botnet Lesson
In 2016, malware called **Mirai** scanned the public internet for IoT security cameras and DVRs using default factory passwords. Within weeks, Mirai enslaved over 600,000 compromised IoT devices into an army (a *botnet*). The attackers commanded this army to flood DNS provider Dyn with massive Distributed Denial of Service (DDoS) traffic, knocking Twitter, Netflix, Reddit, and Spotify offline across North America for hours.

<Callout type="exam">
**The Gold Standard Architecture:** Never place IoT devices on the same internal subnet or VLAN as your corporate servers, databases, or client workstations. Always isolate IoT devices onto a **dedicated, firewalled IoT VLAN** that only has outbound access to the internet, with zero ability to communicate with internal corporate machines.
</Callout>

## Why This Matters in the Field

When you install a commercial smart lock system, deploy IP security cameras for an office building, or set up environmental sensors in a server room, you are introducing network endpoints that attackers will attempt to compromise.

Treating every IoT device as an untrusted endpoint—changing default credentials immediately, disabling UPnP, and isolating them onto dedicated VLANs—is what separates competent network technicians from the people who make headlines for data breaches.
