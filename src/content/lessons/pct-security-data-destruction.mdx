## Thirty Laptops and a Recovery Tool

A school replaces thirty laptops and donates the old ones to a community group. Before they go, a member of staff formats each drive and reinstalls Windows. Reasonable, thorough, and what most people would do.

Months later someone in the community group runs a free file-recovery tool out of curiosity and recovers safeguarding records, staff correspondence, and scanned passports.

Nothing failed. Formatting a drive was never what anyone assumed it was.

## Why Deleting Does Not Delete

On a traditional drive, deleting a file **removes its directory entry** and marks the space as available. The contents stay exactly where they were until something happens to write over them, which may be next week or never.

A standard **quick format** does something similar at a larger scale: it rewrites the file system's structures so the drive appears empty, without touching the data area. The files are all still there; only the index describing them is gone.

Free recovery tools reconstruct files from deleted and formatted drives routinely. This is not a specialist attack — it is the same software used legitimately every day to recover from accidental deletion.

**Sanitisation** is the word for making data genuinely unrecoverable, and it requires either **overwriting** every location or **destroying** the medium.

## Choosing a Method

**Wiping** (overwriting) writes patterns across the entire drive, replacing the data. Its advantage is that the **hardware survives and can be reused**, which matters for cost and for waste. It takes time proportional to drive size.

**Degaussing** applies a strong magnetic field that destroys the magnetic structure of the medium — effective on hard drives and tapes, and it destroys the drive as a working device. **It does nothing to an SSD**, because flash memory does not store data magnetically. This distinction is examined directly and is a genuine field mistake.

**Physical destruction** — shredding, drilling, crushing, incinerating — is the most certain method and ends any possibility of reuse. It is the normal answer for highly sensitive or regulated data, where certainty outweighs the value of the hardware.

The choice depends on **the medium** and **the sensitivity of the data**. Wiping a drive holding ordinary business files before reuse is proportionate. Wiping a drive holding health or safeguarding records and then donating it usually is not — those go to destruction.

## SSDs Change the Answer

Solid state drives complicate wiping. **Wear levelling** deliberately spreads writes across cells to prolong the drive's life, and it keeps spare capacity in reserve. An overwrite therefore cannot guarantee it reached **every** cell that once held data — some of it may sit in blocks the controller has retired from the addressable pool, untouched by anything the operating system can write.

The correct software approach for an SSD is the manufacturer's **secure erase** (or sanitise) command, which instructs the drive's own controller to clear all cells including the reserved ones. Where the data is sensitive enough, physical destruction remains the certain answer.

**Full disk encryption changes the problem usefully.** If a drive has been encrypted since it was first deployed, destroying the encryption key renders everything on it unreadable regardless of what remains in the cells — sometimes called crypto-erase. This is a strong argument for encrypting devices from day one rather than at disposal: the decision made at deployment is what makes retirement simple.

<Callout type="exam">
Two facts do most of the work. **Formatting and deleting do not remove data** — expect scenarios where they were relied upon and the answer is that files remain recoverable. And **degaussing works only on magnetic media**, not SSDs. Match method to medium and to sensitivity: wiping for reuse, degaussing for magnetic media being retired, physical destruction for regulated or highly sensitive data.
</Callout>

## Proving It Happened

Where a third party carries out destruction, a **certificate of destruction** records what was destroyed, by which method, and when — usually with serial numbers.

This is the evidence that the obligation was met. Regulations covering health, financial, and student data require the disposal to have happened, and "we sent them to a recycler" is not a defensible answer without documentation. Keep the certificates with your asset records.

An **asset inventory** is what makes the whole process work. You can only prove you destroyed the drives you know about, and the machine that quietly went into a cupboard three years ago is the one that turns up on an auction site.

<Callout type="warn">
The most expensive assumption in disposal is that a formatted drive is an empty drive. It survives because it looks completely convincing — the drive is genuinely blank in every way a normal person can check, and the person doing it is being conscientious rather than lazy. Donated school laptops, resold office machines, and returned leased equipment have all leaked serious personal data this way, sometimes years later and always publicly. Before any drive leaves your control, decide deliberately: wipe with a tool that overwrites, run secure erase on an SSD, or destroy it. Formatting is not on that list.
</Callout>

## Why This Matters in the Field

Disposal is the last thing anyone thinks about and one of the easiest ways for an organisation to end up in the news. It is also entirely preventable with a routine: every drive leaving the building is wiped, secure-erased, or destroyed, and the decision is recorded against the asset.

The technician usually owns that routine, because the technician is the last person to touch the hardware.
