## The Fix That Wasn't

A help desk ticket comes in: users on the second floor can't reach the internet. You walk upstairs, check a workstation, confirm it can't browse. You notice the default gateway is unreachable. The uplink from the second-floor switch to the core — must be the cable. You grab a new patch cable, swap it out, and head back downstairs feeling efficient. Ten minutes later, another ticket: second floor still can't reach the internet. You go back. Same problem. You check the switch — the uplink port is err-disabled because someone plugged a loop into the VLAN an hour ago. The cable was never the problem. You just wasted twenty minutes, a patch cable, and your credibility because you skipped straight from "identify symptoms" to "implement the first fix that popped into your head."

Troubleshooting without a methodology is just guessing with extra steps. The CompTIA Network+ exam tests a specific seven-step process, and the reason it exists isn't because someone wanted to make your life bureaucratic — it's because unstructured troubleshooting costs time, money, and sometimes makes the problem worse.

## The Seven Steps

Here they are. Memorize them. More importantly, understand why each one exists.

| Step | Action |
|------|--------|
| 1 | Identify the problem |
| 2 | Establish a theory of probable cause |
| 3 | Test the theory to determine the cause |
| 4 | Establish a plan of action to resolve the problem |
| 5 | Implement the solution or escalate |
| 6 | Verify full system functionality and implement preventive measures |
| 7 | Document findings, actions, and outcomes |

Seven steps. In order. Every time. The order isn't arbitrary — each step produces information that the next step requires. Skip a step, and you're building on assumptions instead of evidence.

## Step 1 — Identify the Problem

This is where most troubleshooting fails, and it fails because technicians start solving before they finish understanding. Step 1 isn't "glance at the ticket and start fixing." It's information gathering, and it takes longer than you think it should.

**Gather information.** What exactly is happening? What isn't happening that should be? When did it start? Who is affected — one user, one department, everyone? What changed? That last question is the golden one. Networks don't spontaneously break. Something changed — a configuration push, a firmware update, a new device, a cable getting kicked, a power surge, a DHCP lease expiring. Finding that change often points directly at the cause.

**Question users.** Users describe symptoms, not causes. "The internet is down" might mean their browser can't reach Google, or their email client is throwing errors, or a single internal application times out. These are very different problems with very different causes. Ask specific questions: Can you reach anything on the network? Can you reach internal resources but not external? Did it work earlier today? Did you change anything? The answers narrow the scope.

**Duplicate the problem.** If a user reports intermittent connectivity loss, try to reproduce it. If you can trigger the failure on demand, you can test fixes on demand. If you can't reproduce it, you're working with secondhand reports and guesswork.

**Identify symptoms.** This means documenting exactly what's happening — not what you think the cause is. "Clients on VLAN 20 receive DHCP addresses but cannot reach the default gateway" is a symptom description. "The router is broken" is a premature conclusion. Stay with symptoms until Step 2.

**Approach the problem with fresh eyes.** Don't bring your last troubleshooting experience into this one. The fact that the last three second-floor outages were caused by a bad uplink cable doesn't mean this one is. Confirmation bias kills troubleshooting efficiency. Every problem gets its own investigation, starting from the evidence.

## Step 2 — Establish a Theory of Probable Cause

Now you form hypotheses based on the symptoms you gathered. Not one hypothesis — multiple, ranked by probability.

If second-floor clients have IP addresses but can't reach the gateway, your theories might include: the uplink port is down, the uplink port is in the wrong VLAN, there's an ACL blocking traffic, the gateway interface is down, there's a spanning tree issue isolating the switch, or the switch's routing table is missing the default route. Each of these would produce the observed symptoms.

**Question the obvious.** CompTIA specifically calls this out. Before you build elaborate theories involving routing protocol convergence failures, check the simple stuff. Is the cable plugged in? Is the port enabled? Is the device powered on? A surprising percentage of network outages trace back to something physically unplugged, administratively shut down, or out of power. The fastest troubleshooters check the obvious first — not because they're unskilled, but because they know the probability distribution. Simple causes are more common than complex ones.

Rank your theories by likelihood. Start with the most probable, then work down. This prevents you from spending two hours on an exotic theory while ignoring the loose cable in the patch panel.

## Step 3 — Test the Theory to Determine the Cause

A theory is a guess until you test it. Step 3 is where you prove or disprove each theory systematically.

If your top theory is "the uplink port is down," you check the port status on the switch. If the port shows up/up, the theory is disproven — move to the next one. If the port shows err-disabled, the theory is confirmed, and you've found the cause. If the port shows up/up but traffic analysis reveals it's on the wrong VLAN, you've disproven the first theory and confirmed a different one simultaneously.

The key discipline is testing without fixing. Don't change the configuration to see if the problem goes away. That conflates testing with implementing, and if the change doesn't resolve the issue, you now have two problems — the original one and whatever your untested change introduced. Test through observation: show commands, ping, traceroute, log review, packet captures. Gather evidence. Confirm the cause. Then move to Step 4.

If all your theories are disproven, go back to Step 2 and develop new ones. If you've exhausted your expertise, this is where escalation happens — not because you failed, but because the problem requires knowledge or access you don't have. Recognizing when to escalate is a skill, not a weakness.

## Step 4 — Establish a Plan of Action

You've identified the cause. Now plan the fix before you touch anything.

This sounds overly cautious for a single err-disabled port. Just re-enable it, right? Maybe. But what caused it to err-disable? If it was a loop, re-enabling the port re-enables the loop, and now you've taken down the VLAN. If it was a security violation from a rogue device, re-enabling the port gives the rogue device access again. The plan needs to address root cause, not just symptoms.

A proper plan includes: what action you'll take, what you expect to happen, what you'll check to confirm success, and what you'll do if it doesn't work (the rollback plan). For a simple port re-enable after removing a loop: disconnect the offending cable, clear the err-disabled state, monitor the port for recurrence, and if it err-disables again, investigate the connected device.

For complex issues — a routing change, a firmware update, a firewall rule modification — the plan should account for change management procedures. Who approves it? What's the maintenance window? Who gets notified? What's the backout procedure if the change doesn't work? Step 4 is where troubleshooting meets change management. On the exam, these intersect frequently.

**Consider the impact.** Will your fix affect users currently on the network? A spanning tree root bridge change might cause a brief reconvergence. Restarting a service drops active connections. Rebooting a switch drops everyone on that switch. Schedule appropriately, notify affected users, and have the rollback plan ready.

## Step 5 — Implement the Solution or Escalate

Execute the plan from Step 4. Not a different plan. Not an improvised variation you thought of while walking to the server room. The plan you wrote down, evaluated, and approved.

If the implementation requires skills, credentials, or access you don't have, escalate. If the fix involves core infrastructure that's above your authorization level, escalate. Escalation isn't giving up — it's routing the problem to the person best equipped to resolve it. A junior tech who recognizes that a BGP configuration issue needs the senior network engineer saves more time than one who spends three hours trying to learn BGP in production.

During implementation, document what you're doing in real time. Before and after configs. Timestamps. Exact commands entered. If something goes wrong during implementation, this documentation lets you roll back precisely.

## Step 6 — Verify Full System Functionality

The fix is in. The port is back up. Second-floor users can browse the internet. Done? Not yet.

**Verify that the original problem is resolved.** Can users reach the internet? Can they reach internal resources? Check from multiple clients, not just the one you tested from.

**Verify that the fix didn't break anything else.** Did re-enabling that port introduce a broadcast storm on a different VLAN? Did the spanning tree topology change cause another port to block? Are other services that traverse that switch still functioning? Tunnel vision on the original problem while ignoring collateral damage is how you turn one ticket into five.

**Implement preventive measures.** What stops this problem from happening again? If a loop caused the err-disabled port, enable BPDU guard and loop guard on access ports. If an unauthorized device triggered a port security violation, review the port security configuration and ensure it's applied to all access ports. If a misconfiguration caused a routing black hole, add the correct entry to the configuration template so it doesn't get missed on the next deployment.

Step 6 is where good technicians separate from adequate ones. Adequate techs fix the problem and close the ticket. Good techs fix the problem, verify the fix, and prevent recurrence.

## Step 7 — Document Findings, Actions, and Outcomes

The step everyone skips. The step that matters most for the next person who hits this problem — and that person might be you, six months from now, at 2 AM, having completely forgotten what happened.

Documentation should include: the symptoms reported, the cause identified, the troubleshooting steps taken (including theories that were disproven — this saves the next person from repeating them), the solution implemented, verification results, and preventive measures applied. This goes into the ticketing system, the knowledge base, or both.

Good documentation turns individual troubleshooting experience into organizational knowledge. Bad documentation (or no documentation) means every technician reinvents the wheel every time. You'll work with teams where the senior engineer has all the institutional knowledge in their head and nowhere else. When that person leaves, the team is starting from zero. Document so the knowledge survives personnel changes.

The exam specifically tests whether you know that documentation is the final step. It's a free point. Don't miss it.

## Troubleshooting Approaches — Where to Start in the Stack

When the symptoms don't point directly at a specific layer, you need a systematic approach to narrow down where the problem lives. There are three standard approaches, each starting from a different point in the OSI model.

**Bottom-up** starts at Layer 1 (Physical) and works upward. Check the cable. Check the link light. Check the port status. Then Layer 2: is the MAC address in the CAM table? Is the VLAN correct? Then Layer 3: does the device have an IP? Can it reach the gateway? And so on up through the layers. Bottom-up is thorough and systematic — it guarantees you don't miss a lower-layer issue that's masquerading as a higher-layer problem. The tradeoff is speed: if the problem is a DNS misconfiguration (Layer 7), you've checked a lot of irrelevant layers before finding it.

**Top-down** starts at Layer 7 (Application) and works downward. Can the application reach its server? Is DNS resolving? Is the transport layer connection establishing? Top-down is efficient when the symptoms suggest a higher-layer issue — an application error, a DNS failure, a TLS certificate problem. The tradeoff: if the problem is a bad cable (Layer 1), you've wasted time at the upper layers first.

**Divide and conquer** starts in the middle — typically Layer 3. Can the device ping the gateway? Yes? Then Layers 1-3 are probably fine, and you work upward. No? Then the problem is at Layer 3 or below, and you work downward. Divide and conquer eliminates half the stack immediately and is often the fastest approach because most network problems cluster around Layers 1-3. The exam loves this approach for scenario questions.

No approach is universally best. Bottom-up is safest when you have no idea where the problem is. Top-down is fastest when symptoms point high. Divide and conquer is the default for experienced techs who want to narrow the scope quickly. Know all three and when to use each.

## The Cost of Skipping Steps

Every step skipped is a gamble. Skip Step 1 and you're solving the wrong problem. Skip Step 2 and you're guessing. Skip Step 3 and you're implementing untested theories. Skip Step 4 and your fix might cause a bigger outage than the original problem. Skip Step 5... well, you can't skip the actual fix. Skip Step 6 and you declare victory while something else is on fire. Skip Step 7 and the next person starts from scratch.

The most expensive IT mistakes aren't caused by not knowing the technology. They're caused by rushing. A technician who spends ten minutes gathering information and five minutes fixing the problem is faster than one who spends five minutes guessing and forty-five minutes recovering from the three wrong fixes they tried first.

## Why This Matters in the Field

Every experienced network engineer has a story about the time they didn't follow the process. They jumped to a conclusion, implemented a change without a rollback plan, and spent the rest of the night cleaning up the mess. The methodology feels slow when you're under pressure. It's not slow — it's the fastest path that doesn't involve backtracking.

Your first job will involve pressure to fix things fast. Managers want systems back online. Users are calling. Everyone wants you to skip the questions and start fixing. The methodology is your anchor. It keeps you from making the problem worse while everyone watches. Follow the steps. Identify, theorize, test, plan, implement, verify, document. Every time. The techs who last in this industry are the ones who solve problems once instead of creating new ones.
