## "My Data Doesn't Work — But Only at Home"

A user reports that mobile data is broken. It works at the shops, works at their friend's flat, works in the car. At home, nothing loads.

Nothing is wrong with the cellular connection. Their home router has lost its internet feed, and the phone — behaving exactly as designed — connects to the familiar home Wi-Fi and prefers it for data. The phone is dutifully sending every request down a network that goes nowhere, and never falls back to the cellular connection that is working perfectly.

Almost every confusing mobile connectivity call comes from the same root: **a phone has several radios, they are chosen by rules the user cannot see, and the symptom rarely names the radio at fault.**

## The Radios

A modern phone carries **cellular**, **Wi-Fi**, **Bluetooth**, **GPS**, and usually **NFC**. They are separate hardware, separately switchable, and they fail separately. GPS can be perfect while cellular is dead. Wi-Fi can be connected while the internet behind it is gone.

**Airplane mode** switches all of them off in one action. What surprises people is that Wi-Fi and Bluetooth can then be switched back on individually while cellular stays off — which is why it works on a flight offering Wi-Fi.

Airplane mode is also the most effective first troubleshooting step in mobile support, and it is worth understanding *why* rather than treating it as superstition. Toggling it off and on forces every radio to drop its connection and re-register from scratch — a fresh tower attachment, a fresh Wi-Fi association, new addressing. A large share of transient faults are stale connection state, and that is precisely what the toggle clears.

## The SIM Identifies the Subscriber

Signal bars show how well the phone hears a tower. They say nothing about whether the phone is *allowed* to use it.

The **SIM** — physical, or an embedded **eSIM** — identifies the subscriber to the carrier. Without a valid, activated SIM, the phone can sit under a tower with five bars and still make no calls, because it cannot register on the network. That is why "full bars, no service" is a coherent and common symptom rather than a contradiction, and why the SIM is the thing to check when it appears.

**Roaming** means using a carrier other than your own. Domestically it is often included; internationally it can be expensive, which is why data roaming is a separate switch. Turning it off abroad is the difference between an ordinary bill and a memorable one.

## Hotspots and Tethering

A **mobile hotspot** turns the phone into a small Wi-Fi access point sharing its cellular connection. **Tethering** does the same over **USB** or **Bluetooth** rather than Wi-Fi.

Three practical consequences follow:

- It consumes the phone's **cellular data**, which users forget once a laptop starts downloading updates.
- It **drains the battery** hard, because the phone is running two radios at full duty. A hotspot session should be on charge.
- Many plans meter **hotspot data separately** from phone data. When that separate allowance runs out, the hotspot is throttled or stopped while the phone itself keeps browsing normally — producing the odd report that "the hotspot is broken but my phone is fine."

**USB tethering** is the underrated option. It avoids congested Wi-Fi entirely, is usually faster and more stable in crowded places, and charges the phone while it works.

<Callout type="exam">
A+ tests these as scenario recognition. Anchor on three facts: **airplane mode disables all radios**, after which Wi-Fi and Bluetooth can be re-enabled individually. **Full signal bars with no service** points at the SIM or activation, not reception. And a **hotspot** shares cellular over Wi-Fi while **tethering** shares it over USB or Bluetooth — different transport, same idea.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warn">
The instinct that causes real damage is escalating to the carrier or replacing the SIM before checking what the device is actually connected to. It looks decisive, and it starts a slow process — a replacement SIM, a support ticket, sometimes a re-provisioned line — for a phone whose cellular radio was never the problem. Before any of that, look at whether the device is sitting on a Wi-Fi network that has no working internet, and toggle airplane mode to force a clean reconnect. Those two checks cost fifteen seconds and resolve a large share of "no data" calls outright.
</Callout>

## Why This Matters in the Field

Users describe mobile problems by outcome — "the internet doesn't work" — and the outcome is identical across half a dozen unrelated causes. Your job is to find the radio.

Ask where it fails and where it works, because location patterns point straight at Wi-Fi preference. Check whether Wi-Fi is connected to something broken. Confirm the SIM is present and the line active before trusting the bars. Then toggle airplane mode and let everything re-register.
