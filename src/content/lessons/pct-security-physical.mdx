## Two Coffees and a Smile

A man walks up to a secure office door carrying two takeaway coffees and a laptop bag under one arm. An employee badges in ahead of him, sees his full hands, and holds the door. He thanks her warmly and walks into a building he has no authorisation to enter.

Nothing was hacked. The badge reader worked flawlessly and logged a legitimate entry. What let him in was a door that stayed open and a person who did the polite thing.

Physical security is where a great deal of otherwise strong security quietly ends. **A machine an attacker can touch is a machine they can usually own** — boot it from a USB stick, pull the drive and read it elsewhere, plug something into it. Every technical control assumes that the hardware is where you left it and that only the right people have stood in front of it.

## The Layers

Physical security is layered exactly like network security, and the layers are worth naming because the exam names them:

**Perimeter.** Fencing, **bollards** — the short posts that stop a vehicle being driven through a frontage — lighting, and cameras. These work before anyone reaches a door.

**Entry.** **Badge readers**, **key fobs**, **keypads**, locks, guards, and reception desks. This is where a decision about a person gets made.

**Interior.** Locked server rooms and racks, **cable locks** that tether a laptop to a desk, **privacy screens** that stop a passer-by reading a display, and locking drawers for backup media.

Each layer assumes the ones outside it will be beaten eventually. That is the point of having several.

## What a Door Asks You

The same three authentication factors apply at a door as at a login:

**Something you have** — a badge or fob. Convenient, auditable, and *transferable*, which is the problem. A badge can be lost, lent to a colleague "just this once", or cloned.

**Something you know** — a keypad code. The trouble is that knowledge spreads. Codes get shared for convenience, observed over a shoulder, written on a nearby whiteboard, and remain valid long after someone leaves. Worse, a code **identifies nobody**: the log records that the correct code was entered, not who entered it.

**Something you are** — **biometrics**: fingerprint, retina or iris, facial recognition. The strength is that it cannot be handed to someone else. The weaknesses are practical rather than theoretical — readers reject legitimate people with wet or damaged fingers, and a compromised biometric cannot be reissued the way a badge can.

Combining two of these at a door is **multifactor authentication**, and it means precisely what it does at a keyboard. A badge plus a fingerprint means a stolen badge on its own opens nothing.

<Callout type="exam">
Match the control to the threat it addresses. **Bollards** stop vehicles. **An access control vestibule** stops tailgating. **A cable lock** stops opportunistic laptop theft. **A privacy screen** stops shoulder surfing. And classify authentication at a door by the same three factors used everywhere else — a badge is *something you have*, a code is *something you know*, a fingerprint is *something you are*.
</Callout>

## Tailgating, and the Only Thing That Stops It

**Tailgating** — following an authorised person through a door without presenting credentials — defeats badge readers entirely, because the reader was never asked. The door opened for someone legitimate, and it does not know or care how many people walk through.

The structural answer is an **access control vestibule** (older material calls it a mantrap): two interlocking doors forming a small chamber, where the outer door must close before the inner one will open, admitting **one person at a time**. It cannot be held open for a second person, because the physics of the room do not allow it.

Where a vestibule is impractical, the control is policy plus culture — and culture is the hard part. Tailgating is a **social** attack, not a technical one. It works because refusing to hold a door for someone with full hands feels rude, and most people would rather risk a security incident than be rude to a stranger. Policy has to explicitly make it acceptable to say "sorry, you'll need to badge in" — otherwise you are asking employees to be impolite on their own initiative, and most will not.

<Callout type="warn">
The response that quietly makes an organisation less safe is treating a tailgating incident as an individual failure. It is tempting: someone held the door, so remind that person to be more careful. What it actually teaches everyone watching is that the consequence of a security incident is being blamed, so the next person who lets someone in simply does not report it — and you lose the visibility that would have told you the door is a recurring weak point. Fix the door, not the employee. If people keep being let through, the building needs a vestibule or a staffed reception, because politeness is not a control you can patch.
</Callout>

## Why This Matters in the Field

A technician is frequently the person who notices. You are in the server room, the corridors, and the back offices more than most staff. You see the rack door propped open with a chair for airflow, the code written on the frame, the workstation left logged in and unattended in a public area.

Those are findings worth reporting, and they are often more consequential than anything on the network.
