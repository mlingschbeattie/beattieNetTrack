## The Account That Kept Working

An organisation reviews its systems and finds an active account belonging to someone who left fourteen months ago. It has a valid password, it is a member of the finance file share, and the audit log shows it signing in twice in the last year — from an address nobody recognises.

Nothing was breached in any dramatic sense. Somebody left, IT was told on the Friday, and the ticket to disable the account was never closed.

Physical security decides who gets into the building. **Logical security decides what an account may do once it is inside**, and it fails in this same quiet way far more often than it fails to an attack.

## Least Privilege

**The principle of least privilege** is that an account receives the minimum access required for its job and nothing beyond it. Not what might be handy one day, not what a colleague has, not administrator "so we don't have to keep asking".

Its value is about **blast radius**. Accounts get compromised — someone will eventually enter their password into a convincing fake login page, and no amount of training reduces that to zero. What least privilege decides is what the attacker gets **afterwards**. A compromised account with access to one departmental share is an incident. The same compromise on an account with domain administrator rights is a very different week.

Two practices make it workable:

**Grant to groups, not individuals.** Permissions attach to a role — Finance, Year 10 Students, Helpdesk — and people are added to and removed from that role. Granting to individuals one at a time produces a permission set nobody can audit within a year.

**Remove access when roles change**, not only when people leave. Otherwise you get **privilege creep**: someone moves from support to finance to management, gains access at every step, loses it at none, and after five years holds a set of permissions that no single job could justify. Nothing went wrong at any individual step, which is exactly why it goes unnoticed.

## Multifactor Authentication

MFA requires evidence from **different categories** — something you know, something you have, something you are.

The distinction that catches people is that **two things of the same type are not multifactor**. A password followed by a security question is two pieces of knowledge; anyone who phished the first can usually phish or research the second. A password plus a code from your phone is genuinely two factors, because stealing the password does not put the phone in the attacker's hand.

Tokens come in two forms:

**Hard token** — dedicated physical hardware. A key fob showing a rotating number, or a USB security key. Its strength is that it does one job and cannot be compromised by malware on a general-purpose device.

**Soft token** — software producing codes on a device the user already carries, typically a phone authenticator app. Cheaper and easier to deploy at scale, with the trade-off that its security depends on the phone's security.

MFA is the single most effective control against stolen credentials, because it breaks the attacker's business model: the password alone stops being enough.

<Callout type="exam">
Two points recur. First, **multifactor requires different factor types** — expect a scenario offering two passwords, or a password and a PIN, and asking whether it qualifies. It does not. Second, **least privilege** is the answer whenever a scenario describes damage that spread further than it should have, or an account holding access it does not need.
</Callout>

## Access Control Lists

An **ACL** states which users or groups may do what to a specific resource — this folder, this share, this device.

The behaviour worth internalising is that **an explicit deny normally overrides an allow**. If a user is in one group granted read access and another group explicitly denied, the result is denied. This is deliberate — deny is how you carve an exception out of a broad permission — and it produces one of the most common access puzzles you will troubleshoot: a user who "should" have access by every list you can see, blocked by a single deny inherited from a group nobody remembered they were in.

When someone cannot reach something they should, check **every group they belong to**, not just the one that grants the access.

## Leavers Are a Security Control

Offboarding is where logical security is most often left undone, because it is administrative rather than technical and nobody is inconvenienced when it is skipped.

**Disable rather than delete**, at least initially. Disabling stops access immediately while preserving file ownership, mailbox contents, and audit history. Deleting can orphan files whose owner no longer exists and destroy the trail you would need if something surfaces later.

Then work outward from the account: shared credentials that person knew, badge access, VPN certificates, application accounts that do not use central sign-in, and anything with a personal API key.

<Callout type="warn">
The habit that causes the most harm here is granting administrator rights temporarily and never taking them back. It always starts reasonably — an installation needs elevation, the user is blocked, it is 4pm, so they get local admin "until this is sorted". Nobody schedules the removal, nothing visibly changes, and the account keeps rights it stopped needing that afternoon. Months later that account is phished and the attacker inherits administrator on a workstation. If you must elevate, write the removal into the same ticket before you grant it — a temporary permission with no end date is a permanent one.
</Callout>

## Why This Matters in the Field

Most of what a technician does with logical security is small and repetitive: adding someone to a group, working out why a share is inaccessible, disabling a leaver, resetting MFA for a lost phone.

Done consistently, that work is the control. Done inconsistently, it produces the fourteen-month-old account that still opens the finance share.
