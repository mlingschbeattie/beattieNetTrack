## The Phone That Wiped Itself

An employee leaves a company on ordinary terms. A week later their personal phone — the one they had used for work email — resets itself to factory condition in the middle of the afternoon. Years of family photographs are gone. Nobody did anything malicious. IT ran the standard offboarding procedure, which included a remote wipe of the enrolled device, and the enrolment had been for the **whole phone** rather than for the corporate applications on it.

Everything technical worked exactly as designed. What failed was that nobody told the employee, on the day they typed their work password into their own phone, what they were agreeing to.

Mobile email and device management is where technical configuration meets somebody's personal property, and the second half is the part that gets people hurt.

## IMAP or POP3

Two protocols retrieve mail, and they represent genuinely different models.

**IMAP** keeps messages **on the server** and synchronises state to every client. Read something on your phone and it is read on your laptop. Move it to a folder and the folder moves everywhere. The server is the authority and the devices are views onto it.

**POP3** **downloads** messages to one device and, in its traditional configuration, deletes them from the server afterwards. It was designed for a world of one computer and expensive connections.

For anyone who reads mail on more than one device — which is nearly everyone — **IMAP is the correct choice**, and POP3 produces exactly the symptoms you would expect from its design: messages that exist on one device only, unread counts that never agree, mail that vanishes from the webmail because a phone collected it first.

**SMTP** is not an alternative to either. It sends outgoing mail, and is configured alongside whichever retrieval protocol you choose.

## Ports and Encryption

The secure pairings are worth committing to memory, because a mail client that will not connect is almost always a port and encryption mismatch:

| Purpose | Protocol | Secure port | Legacy plaintext |
|---|---|---|---|
| Incoming, synchronised | IMAP | **993** (TLS) | 143 |
| Incoming, download | POP3 | **995** (TLS) | 995's counterpart, 110 |
| Outgoing | SMTP submission | **587** | 25 |

The legacy ports carry credentials in the clear, which on any shared or public network means handing over a username and password to anyone listening.

**Port 25** deserves its own note. It is the port mail servers use to relay to each other, and consumer internet providers block it almost universally to stop compromised machines becoming spam relays. This produces one of the most recognisable support symptoms in email: **receiving works, sending fails, and only from home.** The account is configured for outgoing port 25, the office network permitted it, and the home provider does not. Submission port **587** is the fix.

<Callout type="exam">
Learn the secure ports as a set: **IMAP 993**, **POP3 995**, **SMTP submission 587**, with 143, 110, and 25 as their unencrypted predecessors. The exam favours scenarios over recall — the giveaway for POP3 is mail appearing on only one device, and the giveaway for a port 25 problem is outgoing mail failing on a specific network while incoming works fine.
</Callout>

## MDM and MAM

**Mobile Device Management** manages the **whole device**. Enrolment lets the organisation enforce a passcode policy, require encryption, push configuration and certificates, restrict features, install and remove applications, locate the device, and **remotely wipe** it.

**Mobile Application Management** manages **only the corporate applications and their data**. Work mail lives in a managed container that IT can configure and erase, and the rest of the phone is untouched and invisible to them.

For **BYOD** — where the employee owns the hardware — MAM is usually the proportionate answer. It protects what the organisation is actually responsible for, which is corporate data, without claiming authority over a device it does not own. MDM makes sense on company-owned hardware, where full control is legitimate.

The distinction matters most at the two moments people forget: **when someone leaves**, and **when a phone is lost**. Both trigger a wipe, and the scope of that wipe was decided months earlier at enrolment.

<Callout type="warn">
The failure here is not technical, and it is one a technician can prevent single-handedly. Enrolling a personal phone in full MDM takes about ninety seconds, the user taps Accept without reading, and email starts working — everyone is satisfied. The consequence surfaces months later as a factory reset that takes someone's photographs with it. Say it out loud at the time: this enrolment lets the company erase this entire phone, and if that is not acceptable, we should be using application management instead. A user who understood the trade-off and chose it has been served properly. A user who found out during the wipe has not.
</Callout>

## Why This Matters in the Field

Configuring mobile mail is a five-minute job that you will do constantly. Choose IMAP unless there is a specific reason not to. Use the encrypted ports. When sending fails on one network only, look at port 25 before you look at anything else.

And when the device belongs to the person in front of you, treat enrolment as a conversation rather than a checkbox. The technical part is easy. Making sure they know what they agreed to is the part that matters later.
