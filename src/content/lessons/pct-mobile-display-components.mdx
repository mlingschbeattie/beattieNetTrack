## The Screen That Was Not Broken

A laptop comes in with a black screen. It powers on, the fans spin, the keyboard backlight glows, and Windows makes its startup sound — but the display shows nothing. The obvious diagnosis is a dead screen, and the obvious quote is a new display assembly.

Then you angle a torch at the panel and see the desktop, faintly, exactly where it should be. The panel is working perfectly. What has failed is the light behind it.

That is the whole reason to understand a display as layers rather than as one part. Almost every display fault you will meet is a fault in one specific layer, and the symptoms tell you which one — but only if you know the layers are there.

## The Layers

From back to front, a typical laptop LCD is a **backlight**, then the **LCD panel** that filters that light into an image, and on touch devices a **digitizer** bonded over the front that senses fingers. Around the edges sit the **bezel**, the **webcam and microphone**, and the **Wi-Fi antennas**. Everything connects to the main board through a cable bundle passing through the **hinge**.

An LCD does not produce light. It produces a mask. Without the backlight there is a perfect image with nothing illuminating it — which is exactly what the torch revealed.

## Panel Types and What They Cost You

Three LCD technologies show up in the exam and on spec sheets:

**TN** — Twisted Nematic. Cheapest, fastest response, and the worst colour. Its signature is that the image shifts and washes out as soon as you look at it off-axis, which is why a TN laptop looks wrong to the person sitting beside you.

**IPS** — In-Plane Switching. Best colour accuracy and the widest viewing angles, which is why it dominates anything sold for design work or simply for looking pleasant. Historically slower and pricier, though that gap has largely closed.

**VA** — Vertical Alignment. Sits between the two, with notably strong contrast and deeper blacks than either, at the cost of some response time.

**OLED** is a different technology, not a kind of LCD. Every pixel generates its own light, so there is no backlight in the stack at all. A black pixel is off — producing true black rather than the grey glow of a backlight leaking through a closed LCD shutter. The trade-off is that emitting pixels age, so an image left static for very long periods can leave a faint permanent ghost, which is **burn-in**.

<Callout type="exam">
A+ asks you to match a panel type to a described symptom or requirement. The reliable tells: **colour shifting badly off-angle** is TN. **Wide viewing angles and accurate colour** is IPS. **True black with no backlight** is OLED, and **burn-in** is an OLED-only concern. Also know that an **inverter** belongs to older **CCFL** backlights — modern **LED**-backlit panels have no inverter, so an inverter cannot be the answer on a current laptop.
</Callout>

## Reading the Symptom Back to the Layer

Because the layers are independent, the failure modes separate cleanly:

**Very dim but visible under a torch** — the backlight or its power circuit. The panel and the video path are fine.

**No image at all, no image under a torch** — the panel, the display cable, or the video source. Connecting an external monitor splits this in one step: if the external display works, the laptop's video output is healthy and the fault is in the panel or its cable.

**Image perfect, touch dead** — the digitizer. It is a separate layer and it fails separately, which is why a phone can display flawlessly and ignore every tap.

**Touch works, no image** — the reverse, and users find it unnerving because the device is clearly responding to invisible taps.

**Symptoms that change as the lid moves** — the display cable flexing through the hinge. Every open and close bends that bundle, and after a few thousand cycles a conductor cracks. Flicker, colour bands, or blanking that correlates with lid angle is close to diagnostic.

## Why the Antennas Are in the Lid

The Wi-Fi card sits on the motherboard in the base, but its **antennas** run up into the display bezel. The lid is the highest point on the machine and is clear of the metal chassis, battery, and board that would otherwise block the signal. Two thin antenna wires therefore travel through the hinge alongside the display and camera cables.

This has a practical consequence during repair: replacing a display assembly means routing antenna cables, and a laptop that comes back from a screen replacement with dramatically worse Wi-Fi range usually has a pinched or unreconnected antenna lead rather than a network problem.

<Callout type="warn">
The misleading move here is replacing the whole display assembly on a black-screen call. It is the expensive part, it is the obvious suspect, and swapping it does sometimes fix the symptom — which is exactly what makes it a trap. If the real fault was a hinge cable or a backlight circuit on the board, you have spent the customer's money on a part that was never broken and the fault returns. Spend the two minutes first: shine a torch at the panel, and plug in an external monitor. Those two checks separate backlight from panel from video source before anything is ordered.
</Callout>

## Why This Matters in the Field

Displays are among the costliest parts on a laptop and among the easiest to misdiagnose, because every fault in the stack presents as "the screen is broken." A technician who can say *the panel is fine, the backlight has failed* is offering a different and much cheaper repair than one who quotes an assembly.

Two tools do most of the work: a torch and an external monitor.
