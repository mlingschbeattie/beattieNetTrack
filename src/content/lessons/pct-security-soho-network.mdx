## The Router Nobody Has Logged Into Since Installation

A small business is compromised. The investigation finds the entry point quickly: the router's administration interface was reachable from the internet, still using the manufacturer's default password, running firmware from four years earlier with a documented vulnerability.

Nobody had touched it since the engineer set it up. It was working, so there was nothing to do.

The SOHO router is the least-maintained device on most networks and the one with the largest exposure. It faces the internet directly, and everything else sits behind it.

## The First Five Minutes

Whenever you deploy or inherit a SOHO router, four things come first:

**Change the administrator password.** Default credentials for every consumer model are published in searchable lists. This is the single highest-value change and it takes thirty seconds.

**Update the firmware.** Routers ship with builds already months old, and vendors patch real vulnerabilities. Check at deployment and periodically after — most devices will not tell you an update exists.

**Disable remote management** from the internet unless it is genuinely needed. This is the setting that turns a misconfiguration into a breach: an administration interface reachable from anywhere will be found by automated scanning, and then it is only a question of the password. Where remote access is truly required, put it behind a VPN.

**Disable WPS.** Its PIN can be brute-forced in hours, and recovering it yields the wireless passphrase — so WPS silently nullifies however strong that passphrase is.

Then set **WPA2 or WPA3 with AES** and a long passphrase, and change the default SSID if it names the manufacturer or model, since that tells an attacker which vulnerabilities to try.

## Guest Networks and Segmentation

A **guest network** gives visitors internet access without reaching internal devices — no file shares, no printers, no cameras, no NAS.

The detail that gets missed: **a separate SSID does not by itself separate anything.** Many routers will happily broadcast a second network name that lands on the same internal network. **Client isolation** or guest isolation is the setting that actually enforces it, and it needs checking rather than assuming.

The same logic applies to **smart devices** — cameras, thermostats, televisions, doorbells. They are frequently built cheaply, rarely updated, and often reachable in ways their owners never intended. They belong on the guest network or their own segment, not sitting beside the machine holding the accounts. If one is compromised, segmentation decides whether that is an annoyance or an incident.

<Callout type="exam">
Know the SOHO hardening list as a set: **change default credentials**, **update firmware**, **disable WPS**, **disable remote management**, **use WPA2/WPA3 with AES**, **enable guest network isolation**, and **disable UPnP**. Expect scenarios describing a compromised small-office network where the answer is one of these being left at its default.
</Callout>

## Port Forwarding and UPnP

**Port forwarding** deliberately opens a path from the internet to a specific internal device — a game server, a camera, a remote desktop.

Every forwarded port is a service **published to the entire internet**. Automated scanners sweep the whole address space continuously, and a newly exposed service is typically found within minutes, not weeks. From that point it is being probed for known vulnerabilities and weak credentials, permanently.

That does not make port forwarding wrong; it makes it a decision. Forwarding a port to a properly patched service with strong authentication is a considered risk. Forwarding **remote desktop or a router's management interface** is how a large share of small-network breaches begin, and it is nearly always avoidable — a **VPN into the network** gives the same access while exposing only the VPN endpoint.

**UPnP** deserves a specific warning. Universal Plug and Play lets applications on the network **open ports through the firewall themselves**, with no administrator approval and usually no visibility. It exists for convenience — games and consoles use it — and it means the firewall's configuration is being decided by software rather than by anyone. Malware on an internal machine can use it to open a path inward. Disable it unless something genuinely requires it.

<Callout type="warn">
The change that most often creates a serious exposure is a helpful one: a user cannot reach their office PC from home, so the technician forwards the remote desktop port and the problem is solved in two minutes. Everyone is pleased, and the service is now on the internet being credential-guessed around the clock by machines that never get bored. It survives because nothing appears to go wrong — until an account with a weak password gives way, and remote desktop hands the attacker a working session on an internal machine. If someone needs access from outside, put in a VPN. It takes longer, and it does not publish the inside of the network to do it.
</Callout>

## Why This Matters in the Field

Small offices, schools, and homes rarely have anyone whose job is the network. The technician who visits for something unrelated is often the only person who will ever look at the router.

It is worth ten minutes: log in, change the password if it is still default, check the firmware, confirm remote management and WPS are off, look at what is being forwarded and why. That is frequently the most valuable work done on the visit.
