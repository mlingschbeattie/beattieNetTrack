## Common Uses of AI

Artificial intelligence (AI) is no longer a futuristic concept — it is embedded in tools students and technicians use every day. Understanding what AI can and cannot do is a foundational skill for IT professionals in 2026 and beyond.

### What Is AI?

**Artificial Intelligence (AI)** refers to computer systems designed to perform tasks that normally require human intelligence: recognizing patterns, understanding language, making decisions, generating content.

**Machine Learning (ML)** is a subset of AI: systems that improve at tasks by learning from data, without being explicitly programmed for each scenario.

**Key distinction:** Traditional software follows fixed rules written by a programmer. ML systems learn rules from data patterns.

### Common AI Application Categories

**Natural Language Processing (NLP)**
Processing and understanding human language:
- **Voice assistants** — Siri, Alexa, Google Assistant; speech-to-text, intent recognition
- **Chatbots** — customer service bots; automated help desks
- **Spam filters** — classify email as spam or legitimate
- **Translation** — Google Translate, DeepL
- **Sentiment analysis** — analyze customer reviews, social media

**Computer Vision**
Teaching machines to interpret visual information:
- **Facial recognition** — device unlock (Face ID), law enforcement, building access
- **Object detection** — self-driving cars identify pedestrians, signals, obstacles
- **Medical imaging** — AI-assisted cancer detection from X-rays and MRI scans
- **Optical Character Recognition (OCR)** — convert printed text to editable digital text
- **Quality control** — manufacturing line cameras detecting defective parts

**Recommendation Systems**
Predicting what a user wants based on behavior:
- **Streaming** — Netflix, Spotify, YouTube suggest content based on viewing/listening history
- **E-commerce** — Amazon "customers also bought" suggestions
- **Social media** — algorithmic feeds prioritize content based on engagement patterns

**Predictive Analytics**
Using historical data to forecast future outcomes:
- **Fraud detection** — banks flag unusual spending patterns in real time
- **Predictive maintenance** — IoT sensors predict equipment failure before it happens
- **Demand forecasting** — retailers predict inventory needs

**Automation and Robotic Process Automation (RPA)**
- Automating repetitive rule-based tasks: data entry, report generation, invoice processing
- **Workflow automation** — calendar scheduling, email routing, ticket triage

**Security AI**
- **Anomaly detection** — identify unusual network traffic or login patterns
- **Endpoint Detection and Response (EDR)** — behavioral analysis of processes
- **SIEM (Security Information and Event Management)** — correlate security events across systems

### AI Limitations — Important for Exam

| Limitation | Description |
|---|---|
| **Bias** | AI trained on biased data produces biased outputs |
| **Hallucination** | AI generates plausible but factually incorrect information |
| **Lack of true understanding** | AI pattern-matches; it doesn't "understand" like a human |
| **Data dependency** | Models are only as good as their training data |
| **Explainability** | Complex models may not explain why they made a decision |

### Key Exam Points

- AI is already embedded in everyday tools: spam filters, voice assistants, recommendation engines
- **NLP** = understanding/generating human language; **Computer Vision** = interpreting images
- **Recommendation systems** personalize content based on behavior — used in Netflix, Amazon, social media
- **Fraud detection** and **anomaly detection** are security-relevant AI applications
- AI has real limitations: bias, hallucination, and opaque decision-making matter for responsible use
