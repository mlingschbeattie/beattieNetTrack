## A Password Nobody Needed

A café offers Wi-Fi with a password printed on the receipt. A customer connects, checks their bank balance, and thinks nothing of it. The connection is encrypted, the network required a password, and the padlock is showing.

Their traffic went through a laptop in the corner of the room. It was broadcasting the same network name as the café, with a stronger signal, and their phone joined it automatically because it recognised the name from last week.

The café's encryption was configured correctly the entire time. It simply had nothing to do with the question of **which access point the phone was talking to**.

## The Standards, In Order

Wireless encryption has a clear historical progression, and A+ expects you to place them:

**WEP** — Wired Equivalent Privacy. The original, and **thoroughly broken**. Flaws in how it uses its keys let an attacker recover the key in minutes by passively collecting traffic. Passphrase length is irrelevant. WEP on a live network is a finding to escalate, not a setting to strengthen.

**WPA** — the interim replacement, built around **TKIP**. It was designed to run on WEP-era hardware via firmware update, and that compatibility constraint left it weaker than a clean design would have been. Also now deprecated.

**WPA2** — introduced **AES**-based encryption (CCMP) and served as the standard for well over a decade. Still acceptable where WPA3 is unavailable. Its known weakness is that an attacker who captures the connection handshake can attack it **offline** — grinding through candidate passphrases on their own hardware, without touching the network again.

**WPA3** — current. Its main improvement is a redesigned handshake that closes that offline attack, so capturing the handshake no longer yields anything to work on. It also improves protection on open networks.

**TKIP and AES are not interchangeable**, and configurations mixing them exist for backwards compatibility. **WPA2 with AES** is the correct modern setting; WPA2 with TKIP drags the older algorithm forward and weakens the result.

## Personal and Enterprise

Every WPA version comes in two modes, and the difference is about identity rather than strength.

**Personal (PSK)** uses **one shared passphrase**. Everyone types the same string. It is simple and correct for a home, and it has two properties that make it awkward in an organisation: it **identifies nobody** — logs show a device, not a person — and revoking access for one individual means **changing it for everyone**, which is why the passphrase in most workplaces has not changed since installation and is known to every former employee.

**Enterprise** authenticates **each user individually** against a **RADIUS** server, typically using their existing network account. Access is granted and revoked per person, the logs name people, and a leaver is handled by disabling their account like everything else.

**RADIUS**, **TACACS+**, and **Kerberos** are authentication services rather than wireless encryption standards — a distinction the exam tests directly. They answer *who is this user*; WPA2 and WPA3 answer *how is this traffic encrypted*.

<Callout type="exam">
Order the standards **WEP → WPA → WPA2 → WPA3**, and pair them correctly: WPA with **TKIP**, WPA2 and WPA3 with **AES**. Know that **Personal** uses a shared passphrase and **Enterprise** uses per-user authentication via **RADIUS**. When a question asks for the most secure option, choose the newest standard offered with AES — and never choose WEP, whatever else the option says about it.
</Callout>

## Rogue Access Points and Evil Twins

A **rogue access point** is any unauthorised access point on or near your network — sometimes malicious, often an employee who plugged in a cheap router to get better signal at their desk.

An **evil twin** is the deliberate version: an attacker's access point broadcasting a **legitimate network's SSID**. Devices identify networks largely by name, and they remember networks they have joined. Put up an access point called the same thing with a stronger signal, and nearby phones and laptops will connect to it — often automatically, with nobody choosing anything.

This is the part worth being precise about: **your network's encryption cannot prevent this.** WPA3 protects traffic between a client and *your* access point. It has no say over a client that has associated with a different access point altogether. The client is not under attack in a technical sense — it is doing exactly what it was configured to do, with the wrong radio.

**WPS** deserves a specific mention. Wi-Fi Protected Setup exists to make joining easy via a button or an eight-digit PIN, and that PIN is validated in a way that reduces the search space enough to be brute-forced in hours. Recovering the PIN reveals the passphrase — **so a 40-character WPA2 passphrase is worth nothing on a router with WPS enabled**. Disable it.

<Callout type="warn">
The reassurance that gets people hurt is "the network asked for a password, so it is safe." It sounds like exactly the right instinct — password means encrypted, encrypted means private — and it collapses entirely against an evil twin, where the attacker simply configures their access point with the same password everyone already knows from the receipt. The user sees a familiar name, a padlock, and no warning of any kind. On any network you do not control, the password tells you nothing about who is running the access point. Treat public Wi-Fi as hostile regardless: use a VPN, and check that sites are HTTPS rather than trusting the network beneath them.
</Callout>

## Why This Matters in the Field

Wireless configuration is routine technician work, and the defaults are frequently wrong. You will find WPS enabled, TKIP left on for a device retired years ago, guest networks that are not isolated from the internal network, and a passphrase that has been the same since the router was installed.

Fixing those is quick. The harder and more valuable part is explaining to users why the padlock on a café network is not the reassurance they think it is.
